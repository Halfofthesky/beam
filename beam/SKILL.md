# BEAM: source roles

Classify every use of outside material by what the writer does with it, not by what the material is. Framework: Joseph Bizup, "BEAM: A Rhetorical Vocabulary for Teaching Research-Based Writing," *Rhetoric Review* 27.1 (2008): 72–86, https://doi.org/10.1080/07350190701738858.

## Modes

1. **Read**: someone else's text. Map the roles, track posture shifts, and say what the source handling reveals about the argument. No quality verdicts unless asked.
2. **Audit**: the user's own draft. Map, diagnose, flag, and propose revision moves. Do not rewrite the text.
3. **Plan**: a question and/or starting materials, no draft yet. Say which roles are filled, which are empty, and what to look for next.

Infer the mode from the input (the user's draft → Audit; a published text → Read; a list of materials → Plan). Infer the genre and aim of the text, or ask once if they decide the diagnosis; state the assumption either way. Genre decides which profiles are normal.

## The four roles

Assign each role by how the material is used in this passage. Test each one with a question.

- **B — Background.** The writer accepts the material's claims as fact and expects the reader to do the same. This includes facts deployed as evidence for the writer's own assertions. Test: is the claim handled as settled? The writer *relies on* it.
- **E — Exhibit.** Material the writer explicates, analyses or interprets. Anything representable can be an exhibit: a text, data, an image, a case, the writer's own experience. The simplest exhibit is an example illustrating a claim; a rich one needs framing and can bear conflicting readings. Exhibits can support claims or give occasion for them. Test: does the text do work to establish what this material means? The writer *interprets* it.
- **A — Argument.** Material whose claims the writer affirms, disputes, refines or extends. Test: is the source's own claim the thing under discussion? The writer *engages* it; record which of the four moves.
- **M — Method.** Material from which the writer takes a governing concept, key terms, a procedure, or a perspective. Test: does it shape how the writer handles the other materials? The writer *follows* it.

Rules:

- **Evidence is not Exhibit.** Bizup is explicit about this. A study cited for its finding and accepted as fact is B. The same study is E if the writer analyses its design or data, and A if the writer contests its conclusion.
- **Never infer role from genre or from primary/secondary/tertiary.** Bizup calls that scheme antirhetorical; it correlates only loosely with use (tertiary → B, primary → E, secondary → A, sometimes B or M). A systematic review cited for its finding is B, not M.
- **The unit is the citation instance, not the source.** The same source can change role across a text; record the shift. A note holding several citations that do the same job counts as one instance group.
- **Roles shade into one another.** One instance can carry two roles, e.g. E+A when the writer analyses a statement and contests it at once. Record both when the text supports both.
- **Uncited materials count.** Method often enters by a dropped name, a terminology or a style; Background by way of "common knowledge". Detect these and put them in the map.

## Ambiguous roles: flag, don't force

When the text supports more than one reading and nothing in it decides between them, mark the role `?`. Give both readings and say what each one depends on. Do not resolve the ambiguity by guessing intent.

Model case (Bizup on Losey, Rayor & Carter, *Nature* 1999). Note 1 documents the proposition the report goes on to contest: that Bt plants are "generally thought" to have negligible impact on non-target organisms. So it cannot be B.

- It is E if "generally thought" is taken at face value, as an illustration of a commonplace.
- It is A if the phrase is read as a gesture the genre requires. The cited item turns out to be an industry guide promoting Bt corn, and that reading places the report in a commercial as well as a scientific debate.

The ambiguity is itself a finding: say what hangs on it.

## Procedure

1. **Thesis.** State the text's question or thesis in one line, quoted from the text where possible. If none can be found, say so; that is already a finding.
2. **Instance pass.** Go through the text in order. For each instance record: location, source, an anchoring phrase quoted verbatim from the text (short; never a paraphrase), role(s), and what the writer does with it (for A: affirm / dispute / refine / extend).
3. **Uncited pass.** Look for terminology, named concepts and unattributed facts that come from identifiable sources. Add them as rows marked `uncited`.
4. **Posture shifts.** For each source that changes role: where it changes, and whether the text acknowledges the change.
5. **Profile.** Show where each role is present and where it clusters, section by section. Never score by counting sources; Bizup rejects source number as a proxy for rigour.
6. **Diagnosis** (Audit, Plan). Compare the profile against the profiles below, adjusted for genre.
7. **Flags**, each with its location and whether it touches the thesis or is local.
8. **Next steps.** Audit: revision moves tied to the flags. Plan: what to look for next.

For long texts (more than about 40 instances), build the table in a file and give the profile, diagnosis and flags in the reply.

## Profiles

The strongest research-based writing analyses specific exhibits in order to further a conversation embodied in a constellation of argument sources. Bizup's reciprocity rule: if you start from an exhibit, look for argument sources to engage; if you start from argument sources, look for exhibits to interpret.

| Profile | Risk | Move |
|---|---|---|
| E without A | Halpern's "complexity thesis": an intricate analysis that announces things are not simple but bears on no larger question | Find A sources the exhibit can speak to |
| A without E | Review essay; second-hand feel | Bring in an exhibit the debate has not yet considered |
| B dominant | Report | Find something to interpret or to contest |
| M without E or A | A procedure in search of an application; no exigency, feels contrived | Find the exhibit or the debate the method is for |

Genre adjusts these:

- A review essay is legitimately A-heavy.
- A briefing is legitimately B-heavy.
- IMRAD distributes the roles by section: introduction B and perhaps A; methods M; results E; discussion gives significance and engages A.

Do not fault a text for being what it is meant to be, but do say what its profile limits.

## Flags

- **F1 Contested as background.** A claim handled as settled that the text itself later qualifies, that the field disputes, or that the cited source merely asserts. If the text contests a claim, its source cannot be B.
- **F2 Uninterpreted exhibit.** Material is presented, but the text does no work to establish what it means; the quotation or data is left to speak. Test: skip the quotation. Is the point still made in the writer's own words?
- **F3 Nominal engagement.** An argument source is named (cf., see also, *pace* X, a string citation) but its claim is never stated or engaged. Fine as signposting; a problem when the text's contribution depends on that disagreement.
- **F4 Unreconciled posture.** A source serves as M or B in one place and is disputed as A in another, and the text never addresses the tension. Contrast Rodriguez: he adopts Hoggart's "scholarship boy" as a method, then openly questions both its claims and its explanatory power. That shift is acknowledged, so it is fine.
- **F5 Implicit method.** A governing concept or vocabulary traceable to an identifiable source goes uncited. This is often conventional, so it is not a fault by default; say whether this genre and audience expect the attribution.
- **F6 Second-hand authority.** A B claim whose authority rests on a source that the cited one merely reports, e.g. a news item cited for a study's finding. Flag only when this is visible from the text or the citation, and say what to trace.
- **F7 Ambiguous role.** See above.

F1, F2, F5 and F6 apply Bizup's definitions. F3 and F4 are extensions, not Bizup's own categories.

## Output

Lead with the diagnosis (Audit, Plan) or with what the source handling reveals (Read). Then the flags, then the table, then the posture shifts. No closing summary.

Use B / E / A / M in every output language, with `+` for a dual role and `?` for an ambiguous one. In Russian prose: фон, экспонат, аргумент, метод.

## Don't

- Rewrite the text. Propose moves; edit only on explicit request.
- Count sources as a measure of quality.
- Classify by source genre or by primary/secondary/tertiary.
- Treat "evidence" as Exhibit by default.
- Force a single role where the text supports two readings.
- Penalise uncited background or method that the genre treats as conventional.
